# RIBAT — Research Problem, Impact and Entrepreneurial Potential

## 1. In which field does your research question fall?

RIBAT’s research question falls primarily within **financial technology and responsible digital finance**, with strong links to:

- **Receivables finance and supply-chain finance**;
- **Financial risk management and fraud prevention**;
- **Digital identity, electronic invoicing and data integrity**;
- **Privacy-preserving computing and cybersecurity**;
- **Distributed systems and trusted public infrastructure**;
- **Commercial law, assignment of receivables and evidence**;
- **SME finance and financial inclusion**;
- **RegTech and supervisory technology**.

The central research question can be stated as:

> How can financial institutions verify whether an invoice or bill of exchange has already been pledged or financed, while preserving commercial confidentiality and creating reliable evidence of registration priority?

The project is therefore not primarily a blockchain or cryptocurrency project. Hedera is considered as a possible public ordering and audit layer. The core research domain is **privacy-preserving integrity infrastructure for receivables finance**.

## 2. Where does the problem primarily lie?

The problem primarily lies at the intersection of **fragmented financial information, weak coordination between competing lenders, privacy constraints and uncertain priority evidence**.

A lender may have a legitimate commercial relationship with a supplier or buyer, but it often cannot see whether the same economic receivable has already been discounted, assigned, pledged or presented to another lender. Each institution typically sees its own portfolio and uses its own systems.

The problem is especially concentrated in the following operational points:

1. **Before financing:** the lender must decide whether to advance money against a receivable.
2. **During verification:** identifiers may be inconsistent, incomplete or changed when an invoice is reissued.
3. **Between institutions:** lenders need a shared answer but do not want to share full invoices, customer relationships, amounts or portfolios.
4. **At the point of priority:** the market may not have a neutral, independently verifiable record showing who registered first.
5. **During disputes:** freezing or challenging a claim may depend too heavily on one operator or one institution.
6. **At the legal and regulatory layer:** a technology record does not automatically create enforceable priority, so the operating model must connect technical evidence with contracts and applicable law.

In simple terms, the problem is not that the data does not exist. The problem is that the relevant data is **distributed, sensitive, inconsistently represented and not coordinated through a trusted neutral process**.

## 3. Who is directly affected?

### Directly affected businesses

**Banks and bank discount desks** are directly exposed because they finance invoices and other short-term receivables. Their risk, credit, operations, compliance and legal teams must decide whether a receivable is sufficiently clear to fund.

**Factoring companies** are directly affected because their business depends on purchasing or advancing against receivables at scale. Duplicate assignment or undisclosed prior pledges can create direct financial losses.

**Leasing and specialty-finance companies** may face similar issues when receivables or payment instruments support financing decisions.

**Supply-chain-finance providers and fintech lenders** need reliable verification while trying to keep onboarding and transaction costs low.

**Large debtors and corporate accounts-payable teams** are affected when the same obligation is presented by multiple parties or when conflicting claims disrupt payment instructions.

### Directly affected people

**Credit officers and risk managers** must decide whether the transaction can be approved and may be held accountable if a duplicate claim is financed.

**Operations officers** perform data entry, documentation checks, registration and release procedures. They bear the cost of slow and manual processes.

**SME owners and finance managers** may lose access to working capital, wait longer for funding or face higher pricing because lenders apply broad risk discounts.

**Treasurers and CFOs of larger buyers** may face disputes over who should be paid and whether a receivable has been validly assigned.

**Legal, compliance and recovery teams** become involved when a claim is disputed, frozen or challenged after funding.

### Wider stakeholders

**Insurers and guarantee funds** are affected because uncertainty about receivable integrity increases underwriting and claims risk.

**Regulators and public financial institutions** are affected because they need reliable market visibility without receiving every institution’s confidential portfolio.

**Guardians, arbitrators, lawyers and other independent professionals** may be needed to review disputed claims and approve a controlled freeze process.

**Technology and e-invoicing ecosystem providers** such as accounting platforms, e-invoice operators and identity or signing providers may become integration partners.

## 4. How many people are potentially affected?

A precise number is **not yet established** and should not be invented. The first validation task is to obtain reliable data from lenders, factoring companies, regulators and SME-finance organisations.

The affected population exists at three levels:

### Primary institutional population

The primary population consists of:

- banks and their lending, risk, operations and legal teams;
- factoring companies;
- leasing and specialty-finance companies;
- supply-chain-finance providers;
- insurers and guarantee funds;
- large corporate debtors and accounts-payable departments.

Even when the number of institutions is limited, each institution may have many employees involved in origination, verification, approval, operations, recovery, legal review and audit.

### Business population

The wider business population includes SMEs that depend on invoice financing, factoring, trade finance or short-term working capital. The number of potentially affected SMEs should be estimated using official Tunisian data and validated against the portfolios of participating lenders.

For the project, the most honest current statement is:

> Potentially affected businesses include the entire population of SMEs and corporate debtors using receivables finance, plus every lender and professional involved in originating, verifying, funding, insuring, administering or disputing those receivables. The exact number, annual financed value and number of affected transactions remain unvalidated research variables.

### Transaction population

The most relevant measure may not be the number of people. It may be:

- number of receivables financed annually;
- total value of receivables financed;
- number of lenders and factors active in the market;
- number of duplicate or conflicting claims;
- staff hours spent checking each transaction;
- number and value of losses or delayed recoveries.

RIBAT should not claim a large affected population until these figures are sourced and confirmed. A small number of institutions can still represent a large economic opportunity if they process high-value receivables at scale.

## 5. Causes of the problem

The problem exists because several causes reinforce one another.

### Fragmented information

Receivable records are held by different lenders, SMEs, buyers, accountants, e-invoice platforms and public or private systems. No single participant necessarily sees the complete financing history.

### Inconsistent identifiers

The same economic obligation may be represented using different invoice numbers, formatting conventions, dates, references or document versions. A reissued invoice may not look identical to the earlier record.

### Competitive confidentiality

Lenders do not want to expose their supplier relationships, customer relationships, financing amounts or portfolio composition to competitors. A solution that requires full data sharing may be commercially unacceptable.

### Manual and expensive verification

Checks may depend on documents, emails, confirmations, spreadsheets, internal calls and legal review. These processes are slower and more expensive for small-ticket receivables.

### Weak shared priority evidence

Even when a lender has internal records, other institutions may not accept them as neutral evidence. The market needs a common mechanism for recording when a claim was submitted and what status it had at that time.

### Instrument and process substitution

When one payment or financing instrument is restricted or reformed, risk may move to another instrument. A control system focused on only one document type may leave gaps.

### Governance and dispute uncertainty

A disputed claim needs more than a technical flag. Participants need a fair process defining who may propose a freeze, who approves it, how long it lasts and how the decision can be challenged.

### Legal and regulatory uncertainty

Technology can create evidence, but it cannot independently determine assignment validity, enforceability or insolvency priority. Without an appropriate legal and institutional framework, technical registration may have limited practical value.

## 6. What are the consequences?

### Financial consequences

- Duplicate financing can create direct credit or recovery losses.
- Lenders may apply higher haircuts, prices or collateral requirements.
- Capital may be reserved against uncertainty rather than productive SME activity.
- Insurers and guarantee funds may price the risk conservatively.

### Operational consequences

- Credit approval takes longer.
- Staff spend time searching, reconciling and validating documents.
- Small transactions may become uneconomic to process.
- Disputes consume legal, recovery and management resources.

### Consequences for SMEs

- Working capital may arrive too slowly.
- Eligible receivables may be rejected because they are difficult to verify.
- Financing may become more expensive.
- Smaller businesses may receive less access than larger businesses with stronger documentation and relationships.

### Consequences for debtors

- A debtor may receive conflicting payment or assignment instructions.
- Payments may be delayed while competing claims are resolved.
- The debtor may face legal or operational risk even when the underlying obligation is genuine.

### Market consequences

- Competition between lenders is weakened by low trust.
- Institutions may keep larger safety margins than necessary.
- The market may lack reliable aggregate information about prevented fraud, disputes and recovery outcomes.

## 7. What is the potential for impact?

RIBAT could create impact in five connected ways.

### Preventing avoidable losses

If the system detects a duplicate or conflicting claim before funding, the lender can stop, investigate or require additional evidence. The value of the system depends on the number and value of such events, which must be measured in a pilot.

### Lowering verification costs

A fast, standardised and privacy-preserving check could reduce manual work and make smaller receivables more economical to process.

### Improving SME access to finance

If lenders can reduce uncertainty without demanding excessive documentation or applying broad discounts, more receivables may become financeable.

### Creating auditable dispute handling

A guardian and time-lock process can reduce unilateral action and make freeze decisions easier to review. The process must be legally designed and independently governed.

### Enabling better market infrastructure

A shared, redacted event history can support insurers, guarantee funds, regulators and auditors without exposing the underlying commercial portfolios.

The potential impact is therefore significant **if** the problem is frequent, the legal model is credible and multiple institutions participate. It is not yet possible to quantify the impact responsibly without baseline data.

## 8. Why might this issue create an economically viable and sustainable solution?

### The problem is connected to financial loss

A solution that prevents even a small number of high-value duplicate-financing incidents may create more value than its operating cost. This must be demonstrated with actual lender data rather than assumed.

### The users already have budgets for risk and operations

Banks, factors, leasing companies and insurers already spend money on credit assessment, fraud controls, compliance, legal review and recovery. RIBAT could become viable if it reduces a measurable cost or loss within an existing budget.

### The service can be sold as infrastructure, not speculation

The product does not need a token, crypto investment or consumer adoption. It can be sold as a B2B verification, registry, evidence and risk-management service.

### Network effects may increase value

A registry becomes more useful as more relevant lenders participate. One lender can use it for internal controls, but multiple participating institutions create the cross-lender visibility that makes duplicate detection more valuable.

### Revenue can be diversified

Potential revenue streams include:

- per-check or per-registration fees;
- lender subscriptions;
- API and portfolio dashboards;
- attestation and debtor-confirmation services;
- audit and evidence exports;
- anonymised risk analytics;
- insurer or guarantee-fund partnerships.

However, insurance or warranty revenue should not be treated as a near-term assumption. It requires licensed partners, validated loss data and a clear allocation of liability.

### The system can begin with a narrow, measurable use case

RIBAT does not need to solve all receivables finance at once. It can begin with one instrument, one lender consortium, one jurisdiction and one measurable event: detecting duplicate or conflicting financing before an advance is made.

### Sustainability depends on trust and governance

A sustainable solution needs more than software. It requires:

- neutral governance;
- clear member agreements;
- privacy and cybersecurity controls;
- legal review;
- transparent pricing;
- an incident and correction process;
- reliable service levels;
- a credible exit and data-retention policy.

The technology can support these conditions, but cannot substitute for them.

## 9. The most important validation questions

Before building the production system, RIBAT should answer:

1. How many duplicate or conflicting receivable-financing cases do target institutions experience each year?
2. What is the average and total value of the associated losses or delayed recoveries?
3. How much does the current verification process cost per transaction?
4. Who owns the budget for solving the problem?
5. Will lenders participate in a shared registry with competitors?
6. What minimum data can legally and operationally be shared?
7. What evidence would a lender, court, insurer or regulator accept?
8. Can a legal agreement make the recorded priority meaningful?
9. Will at least three institutions provide anonymised data or join a pilot?
10. What price would make the service economically sustainable?

## 10. Concise answer for an application form

**Field:** Financial technology, receivables finance, fraud prevention, privacy-preserving digital infrastructure and RegTech.

**Where the problem lies:** The problem lies in the absence of a neutral, privacy-preserving mechanism that allows lenders to detect whether the same invoice or bill of exchange has already been pledged or financed and to verify registration priority.

**Who is affected:** Banks, factoring companies, leasing companies, fintech lenders, credit and operations teams, SMEs seeking working capital, large corporate debtors, insurers, guarantee funds, regulators and professionals involved in disputes and recovery.

**How many are affected:** The exact number is not yet validated. The potentially affected population includes the institutions and employees processing receivables finance, the SMEs and debtors behind those receivables, and the professional and public stakeholders responsible for risk, evidence and disputes. The correct next step is to measure transaction volume, financed value, duplicate cases and verification cost through a lender pilot.

**Causes:** Fragmented records, inconsistent invoice identifiers, reissued instruments, manual checks, competitive confidentiality, weak shared priority evidence and legal/governance uncertainty.

**Consequences:** Duplicate-financing losses, slower and more expensive credit decisions, higher risk pricing, reduced SME access to working capital, payment disputes and inefficient use of lender and regulatory resources.

**Potential for impact:** RIBAT could reduce preventable losses, lower verification costs, improve the financeability of smaller receivables, create auditable dispute handling and provide better aggregate information to insurers and regulators. The impact must be quantified through real market data.

**Entrepreneurial potential:** The problem may support a sustainable B2B infrastructure business because the target customers already spend money on risk controls, operations, compliance and recovery. RIBAT could charge for checks, registrations, APIs, subscriptions and evidence services. Its viability depends on proving that the problem is frequent and costly, obtaining legal acceptance, securing participation from multiple institutions and demonstrating willingness to pay.
